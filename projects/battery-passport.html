Does a battery passport need AI at all?

Team of eight · Feb to May 2026

Question
Which parts of an EU battery passport system actually need AI, and which are better left to rules?
What we built
A proposed compliance architecture for EV-industry companies, in two versions for different types of customer.
My part
The problem definition, the solution architecture and the competitive landscape. I assessed language model options, compared the two versions, and co-built the cost comparison.
Key idea
AI only where there is no fixed right answer. Checks that have a right answer go to rules, and final submissions go to a person.

The problem

From 18 February 2027, every EV battery and every industrial battery over 2 kWh sold in the EU needs a digital battery passport. To fill one in, a manufacturer has to collect data from dozens of suppliers, in different file formats, with different names for the same values, and often in different languages. Most of that work is still done by hand.

Our brief was to propose how AI and data management could make it manageable.

Context & constraints

A TIAS student consultancy project with Accenture Netherlands, with eight students. I owned the problem definition, solution and competitive landscape sections, co-built the cost model with two teammates, and presented the first part of the final pitch. Other teammates covered target customers, the roadmap and the commercial model.

The full proposal is not public, so this page covers my reasoning and leaves out figures and deliverables.

How the thinking moved

  1. Is this overengineered?
  2. Where does AI belong?
  3. Does each part earn its place?
  4. What's the minimum that complies?
  5. Which version for which customer?
  6. Does it all hold together?

Early on, I was leaning toward a stack built on small language models and blockchain. An engineer we spoke to described most of it as "essentially just BI." That sent me back through the stack to ask, for each component, what problem it solved that something simpler couldn't.

Judgment calls

  • Where AI belongs. Reading supplier documents is the hard part. The same value can be "Nominal V" in one file and "Rated Voltage" in another, in English in one and Chinese in the next. No list of rules covers that, so this is where a language model earns its place. Checking whether a value meets the regulation is different: the regulation defines the answer, so SQL rules and data quality checks handle it, and errors are flagged back to the supplier automatically. A model there would only add the risk of a confident wrong answer.

  • Matching models to jobs. We kept to open-weight models that could run on a company's own servers. Phi-4 handles structured extraction, Llama is the fallback for documents in other languages, and Mistral is an optional add-on for spotting anomalies. The validation layer itself has no language model in it.
  • Segmenting by customer, not by feature. Both versions do the same things; the difference is how they're built. Large cell manufacturers, with many suppliers and messy data, get three separate layers that are easier to scale and to swap parts out of later. Tier-1 suppliers with simpler data get two, with capture and validation merged, which is quicker to deploy and has fewer parts to maintain.
  • On-premise by default. Battery chemistry is commercially sensitive, and suppliers won't share it if it leaves their control. Everything runs on the company's own servers, and the passport shares proof that the data complies rather than the data itself.
  • Costing the architecture, not the model. With two teammates, I compared what each version would cost to build and run over five years. We compared architectures because that's the decision a customer actually faces. Open-weight models carry no licence or per-use fees, so cost comes mostly from the hardware to run them and the people to maintain the system, and swapping one model for another barely changes it. The versions differed mainly in integration work and the size of the team each needs, which we modelled separately for Western and Eastern European salaries.
  • Checking that it holds together. Before the final version, I went back through the proposal: whether both versions really included the same proof layer, whether cost line items added up to the totals, and whether external figures had a source. Several didn't, and were fixed.

Looking back

Agree on what each component must justify, early. My early evaluation found blockchain was only needed to notarise compliance results. As workstreams developed in parallel, the final proposal made it a core layer. Next time I'd agree up front on what each component has to justify before it goes into the architecture, and check against that as the work comes together.

Screen models on licence and language first. Llama 4's licence excludes companies based in the EU, and Chinese is not among its officially supported languages, even though most battery suppliers are in Asia. A licence and language check before comparing capabilities would have changed the shortlist.

Test on real documents. We compared models on what they're designed for, not on actual supplier files. A small test set of real multilingual documents would have been the first step.
