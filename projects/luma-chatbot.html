How do you get a customer to finish giving feedback?

IBM Watson Assistant · 2026 · Team of four

Play the demo →
Question
How do you collect feedback after a purchase or a return, when most customers would rather skip it?
What we built
A tap-to-answer feedback chatbot for Luma, a fictional cosmetics brand, that ends in a spin-wheel reward.
My part
Co-designed the conversation flow, and created the visual identity, chatbot imagery and spin-wheel page.
Key idea
Each answer option points to the team that needs to act on it.

The problem

Feedback after a purchase, and especially after a return, is some of the most useful information a brand can get. It is also the hardest to collect. Once a customer has their product, or has sent it back, there is little reason to answer a survey about it.

For an AI literacy course taught with IBM, our team designed a feedback chatbot for Luma, a fictional cosmetics brand. The goal was to make giving feedback quick enough to finish, and worth finishing.

Context & constraints

This was a team project with four people. I co-designed the conversation flow with one teammate, and created Luma's visual identity, the chatbot imagery and the spin-wheel reward page.

The chatbot was built in IBM Watson Assistant on a trial account, which has since expired. The playable demo recreates the same flow in HTML and JavaScript. It was a course prototype, tried out by classmates rather than real customers.

The flow

Customers first choose what to give feedback on. Each path is a few tap-to-answer questions, followed by one optional open question and the spin wheel.

Recent purchase

  1. How did the product meet your expectations?
  2. What did you like most, or what was the main issue?
  3. How was the delivery?
  4. What could be improved? (only if delivery wasn't good)
  5. Would you recommend it?

Refund or return

  1. What was the main reason for the return?
  2. How was the return process?
  3. How was the refund process?
  4. What was the issue with the refund? (only if it wasn't good)

Both paths end with "Anything else you'd like to tell us?" and the spin wheel.

Judgment calls

  • Taps instead of typing. Every question has buttons, so a customer can finish without writing anything. The cost is detail: buttons only capture what we thought to offer. We kept one open question at the end, after the structured ones, so customers with more to say could say it without everyone having to.
  • Follow-up questions only when something went wrong. A good rating moves straight on. An okay or poor rating gets one follow-up asking what the problem was. Satisfied customers finish faster, and the extra questions go to the customers whose answers are most useful.
  • Answer options that route themselves. We wrote the options so each answer already points to a team. For returns, a damaged product or wrong item points to fulfilment, while "didn't like it" and "quality not as imagined" point to the product or how it was presented. The purchase path works the same way: a skin reaction is a product issue, packaging and tracking are delivery issues.
  • Acknowledge before asking more. When a customer reports a problem, Luma responds first ("We're sorry you had to return your product", "We'll look into this and make it right") and only then asks the next question.
  • The reward comes last. Only customers who finish can spin the wheel. All four prizes (15% off, buy one get one free, free shipping, a free gift) apply to the next purchase, so the reward also gives customers a reason to come back.

Looking back

The return path has an inconsistency. A poor refund rating gets a follow-up question, but a poor return rating does not. Customers unhappy with the return process can say so, but not why. I'd add the same follow-up there, or merge the two ratings into one.

We never measured completion. The whole design is a bet that fewer, easier questions and a reward lead more people to finish. With real traffic, the first thing I'd track is where customers drop off, question by question, and whether completion changes with and without the wheel.

An LLM would change the open question. Today, someone would have to read every free-text answer. A language model could tag each one by topic and urgency and send it to the right team, the same way the button options already do. The trade-offs are cost per conversation and answers getting mis-tagged, so I'd start by running it next to human review.
